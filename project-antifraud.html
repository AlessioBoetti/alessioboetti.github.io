01 — Context & Problem

The challenge of fraud at scale

Banking fraud is an adversarial problem: fraudsters continuously adapt their behavior to evade detection. A static rule-based system, however carefully tuned, inevitably becomes stale. The challenge was to build a model-driven detection system that could generalize to unseen fraud patterns while keeping false positive rates low enough to not disrupt legitimate customers.

The data was highly imbalanced (fraud events represent a fraction of a percent of all transactions, <0.01%), with complex temporal and behavioral patterns: the same transaction amount might be perfectly normal for one customer and a strong fraud signal for another. Classical approaches failed to capture this context-dependency.

Additionally, the system needed to operate under strict latency constraints: decisions had to be made in near-real-time within the transaction authorization flow and needed to be fully explainable to fraud analysts and compliance teams.

The pipeline is organized in three layers: offline training (batch data ingestion, feature engineering, model training, hyperparameter optimization, evaluation), online serving (real-time feature lookup from a precomputed database, low-latency inference), and monitoring (fraud analyst decision and evaluation, scheduled retraining triggers).

03 — Feature Engineering

Turning raw transactions into signals

The most impactful work was not model selection, it was feature engineering. Fraud signals are rarely visible in a single transaction, they emerge from patterns over time and across customer context. The choice of which features to engineer was carefully evaluated with statistical methods and empirical rigor. Eventually, several types of features were engineered: RFM-based (Recency, Frequency, Monetary framework), behavioural-based, and more complex and diverse ones.

04 — Model Decisions

Why tree-based gradient boosting models, and how they are tuned

Why tree-based models? Why

Tree-based models handle mixed feature types, missing values, and complex, non-linear interactions natively, all of which are common in transactional data. They also require less preprocessing than neural networks, they are robust to noisy, real-world data, and they provide interpretable decision logic, making them well-suited for high-stakes domains like fraud detection where both accuracy and explainability matter.

Why gradient boosting? Why

Gradient boosting models deliver state-of-the-art predictive performance by combining multiple weak models into a highly accurate system. They are particularly effective at detecting subtle fraud patterns, improving detection rates while keeping false positives low.

Three perspectives How

Although similar, XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM each have different inductive biases and error patterns. Each of them was evaluated in order to find the best model for each antifraud module based on performance and operational fit.

Handling class imbalance How

Instead of oversampling, which introduces artificial data, a mix of proprietary confidential techniques was used to accurately identify rare fraud events without increasing false positives and false negatives. Threshold calibration was done separately after training.

Hyperparameter search How

Bayesian optimization guided the search of optimal model hyperparameters. This cut tuning compute by ~70% while finding better configurations than random search.

05 — Uncertainty in Practice

Making predictions nuanced

Prediction models can output binary labels to indicate the predicted outcome. However, rather than forcing a rigid approve/decline outcome, the system produces risk scores that reflect the likelihood of fraud. This lets fraud analysts or automated systems apply different decision thresholds for different transaction risk profiles and better handle edge cases: a €10 transaction might be blocked at 40% fraud probability, while a €50,000 wire transfer gets flagged at 5%.

06 — Explainability in Practice

Making predictions auditable

In a regulated financial environment, "the model said so" is not acceptable. Every fraud block must be defensible to compliance, to customers who dispute a blocked transaction, and to regulators. SHAP-based explanations were integrated directly into the analyst review workflow, explaining which features drove the prediction. This increases analyst trust, speeds up review time, and provides a feedback loop for feature improvement.
